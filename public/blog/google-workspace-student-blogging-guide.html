A Teacher's Curated Resource Guide to Digital Tools for Online Tutoring

17 July 2026 · 6 min read · By ThinkViva

Teaching online is not simply teaching in front of a camera. It requires a different set of digital skills and a working knowledge of the tools that make remote learning feel structured, engaging, and professional. For tutors who are new to online delivery, or who want to sharpen how they use the tools they already have, this guide is a practical starting point.

Below is a curated list of the core digital tools every online tutor should be comfortable with, along with links to the official tutorials for each one. No unnecessary complexity, just the tools that actually matter for running effective sessions with children.

Who this is for: Online tutors and teachers delivering sessions to primary and secondary students. All tools listed below are free to use with a standard Google account.

1. Google Classroom: Your Session Hub

Google Classroom is the central organiser for any online tutor working with more than one student. It is where you post assignments, share worksheets, collect completed work, leave written feedback, and keep a record of each student's progress, all in one place. Setting it up properly at the start of a tutoring relationship saves considerable back-and-forth later.

Key uses for tutors:

  • Create a separate class for each student or subject group
  • Post assignments before the session so students can arrive prepared
  • Attach Google Docs templates and distribute individual copies automatically
  • Leave private feedback on submitted work that only the student sees
  • Use the Stream to share links, announcements, and session reminders
Google Classroom

Create an assignment in Google Classroom

How to set up an assignment, attach documents, set a due date, and distribute individual copies to each student. The starting point for any structured tutoring workflow.

Google Classroom

Give feedback on student work

Covers leaving private comments, returning marked work, and using the comment bank for frequently used feedback phrases, a significant time-saver across multiple students.

2. Google Docs: Collaborative Writing and Feedback

Google Docs is where the writing work happens. Whether a student is drafting an essay, completing a comprehension exercise, or working through a structured worksheet, Docs gives the tutor full visibility in real time and the ability to leave precise, actionable feedback without overwriting the student's own work.

Key features for tutors:

  • Use Suggesting mode so your edits appear as tracked changes the student can accept or discuss
  • Add comments on specific sentences to explain why something needs to change, not just that it does
  • Share a document with parents on view-only access so they can follow progress without editing
  • Check version history to see exactly what the student changed between sessions
Google Docs

Get started with Google Docs

Creating, editing, sharing, and formatting documents. The essential starting point for any tutor using Docs with students for the first time.

Google Docs

Add, edit, and reply to comments

Step-by-step guide to the comments feature, including resolving comments once a student has acted on them. Share this with students before their first feedback session.

Google Docs

See document activity and version history

How to view previous versions of a document and see what changed. Useful for showing students how much their writing has developed over a term.

3. Google Drive: Organising Your Teaching Materials

A well-organised Drive is what separates a tutor who feels in control of their sessions from one who is searching for files mid-lesson. Create a clear folder structure from the start, one folder per student, with subfolders for each term or subject, and keep all worksheets, lesson plans, and completed work in the same place. Shared folders also make it straightforward to give students and parents access to relevant materials without email attachments.

Google Drive

Organise files with folders in Google Drive

A simple but important guide to creating a folder structure. Getting this right at the start makes every session run more smoothly.

Google Drive

Share files and folders in Google Drive

Explains the difference between sharing with specific people, sharing via link, and setting editor or viewer permissions, foundational knowledge for any tutor sharing materials with students and parents.

4. Google Slides: Visual Lesson Delivery

A well-prepared Slides deck is one of the most effective ways to structure an online tutoring session. Rather than talking over a blank screen, you can share your presentation during a Google Meet call and walk a student through a lesson visually, one slide at a time. Slides are particularly useful for vocabulary lists, worked examples, diagrams, and step-by-step problem solving, anything that benefits from being broken into clear, visual steps.

Google Slides

Get started with Google Slides

Creating a presentation, adding slides, inserting images and text, and sharing. The starting point for any tutor building lesson slides for the first time.

Google Meet

Present and share your screen in Google Meet

How to share a specific tab, window, or your full screen during a Meet session. This is how you display your Slides deck live to a student, letting them follow along as you teach.

5. Google Forms: Quick Checks and End-of-Session Quizzes

Google Forms is underused by most online tutors. A short Form sent at the end of a session, three to five quick questions on what was just covered, gives you an immediate read on what the student understood and what needs revisiting next time. It also builds good habits: students learn to expect a brief check-in and come to sessions knowing they will be asked to recall what they learned.

Practical uses during tutoring:

  • End-of-session comprehension check: 3 to 5 short questions sent in the chat
  • Pre-session warm-up: recap questions on the previous lesson emailed in advance
  • Parent feedback form: a short check-in at the end of each month to see how sessions are landing at home
Google Forms

Create a form in Google Forms

How to build a Form from scratch, add question types (multiple choice, short answer, paragraph), and share the link with students. Setting up a quiz takes under five minutes once you know the steps.

Google Forms

View and manage Form responses

How to review individual responses and see summary results. After a session quiz, you can check results before the next lesson and know exactly where to start.

6. YouTube: Bringing Lessons to Life with Educational Video

YouTube is one of the most powerful but least structured tools in a tutor's kit. Used well, a short, well-chosen video can explain a concept in a way that clicks for a student when the tutor's own explanation has not quite landed. It also breaks the rhythm of a session and keeps younger learners engaged across a screen.

The key is curation, not volume. Find two or three reliable channels for your subject and age group, bookmark the videos that align with your curriculum, and share your screen in Google Meet to play them together rather than just sending a link for students to watch alone. Watching and discussing a short video together, pausing to ask questions, is a far more effective use of three minutes than any amount of passive watching.

Google Meet

Share your screen to play a YouTube video in Meet

Share a specific browser tab in Google Meet to play a YouTube video directly in the session. Students see exactly what you see, including any pausing or rewinding, making it a genuinely shared learning moment rather than a break.

Channels worth bookmarking: Khan Academy (Maths and Science, K to Grade 9), BBC Bitesize (English curriculum and general literacy), TED-Ed (short explainer videos on a wide range of topics for older students), and Crash Course Kids (science for primary age). Keep a shared Drive folder of saved YouTube links organised by topic so you can pull the right video quickly mid-session.

7. Whiteboard: Interactive and Visual Problem Solving

A digital whiteboard changes the feel of an online session. Instead of a tutor speaking at a student, both parties are contributing to the same shared space, drawing diagrams, working through a Maths problem step by step, mapping out a story structure, or simply sketching ideas. For younger learners especially, the ability to draw and annotate together makes abstract concepts far more tangible.

A note on Google Jamboard: Jamboard was shut down on 31 December 2024. The two best replacements are below.

Google Meet

Use the built-in whiteboard in Google Meet

Google Meet has a whiteboard you can open directly from any call, no additional app or sign-up needed. Both the tutor and student can draw, write, and annotate together in real time. The simplest option for tutors who want to get started immediately.

FigJam

FigJam by Figma (free for educators)

A more fully featured whiteboard with sticky notes, shapes, drawing tools, and emoji reactions. FigJam boards are shareable via link with no account required for the student to participate. The free education plan covers individual tutors. A good choice if you want to save boards between sessions and build on previous work.

Practical tip: Open a whiteboard at the start of every session rather than waiting for a moment you need it. Students who know the whiteboard is always available are more likely to use it to show their thinking, which tells you far more about how they are learning than a correct answer alone.

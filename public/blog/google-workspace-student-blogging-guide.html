Google Workspace for Student Blogging: A Teacher's Curated Resource Guide

17 July 2026 · 7 min read · By ThinkViva

Student blogging is one of the most effective writing projects you can run online. It gives learners a real audience, a reason to care about presentation, and a portfolio of work they can look back on. The good news for tutors and teachers is that you do not need any specialist blogging software. Google Workspace has everything you need, and most of your students are already familiar with at least part of it.

This guide pulls together the best official Google tutorials and training links for each tool in the stack, so you can bookmark what you need and share relevant links directly with your students or their parents.

Who this is for: Online tutors, classroom teachers, and after-school programme leads running writing or creative projects with students aged 8 and above. All tools listed below are available free through Google Workspace for Education or a standard Google account.

1. Google Docs: Where Every Blog Post Begins

Before a post goes anywhere public, it should be drafted, revised, and proofread in Google Docs. The commenting and suggestion features make it ideal for tutor feedback, and the version history means you can always revisit earlier drafts to show a student how their writing has improved.

Key features for blogging projects:

  • Leave targeted comments on individual sentences or paragraphs using the comment toolbar
  • Use Suggesting mode so students see your edits before accepting them, rather than receiving a silently corrected document
  • Share a document with view-only access so parents can read progress without editing
  • Use the Outline panel (View > Show outline) to help students structure longer posts with headings
Google Docs

Get started with Google Docs

Official Google Help Centre guide covering creating, editing, sharing, and formatting documents. Good starting point for any student new to Docs.

Google Docs

Add, edit, and reply to comments

Step-by-step guide to the comments feature, including how to resolve comments once feedback has been acted on. Useful to share with students before their first peer review session.

Google Docs

See document activity and version history

Explains how to view previous versions of a document, restore older drafts, and see who changed what. Great for showing writing progress over time.

2. Google Sites: Publishing Student Blogs

Google Sites is the simplest way to give students an actual published blog. It requires no coding knowledge, works on any device, and integrates directly with the rest of Workspace. Students can embed Docs, Slides, YouTube videos, and Drive files all in the same page without switching tools.

How to structure a student blog on Sites:

  • Create one Site per student with a simple home page and a new page for each blog post
  • Use the "Embed" block to pull in a Google Doc directly, so editing the Doc automatically updates the published page
  • Set sharing to "Anyone with the link can view" so parents can access without needing a Google account
  • For younger students, create a shared class blog Site and give each student their own page
Google Sites

Create and edit Google Sites

The main help article for building a new Site from scratch. Covers adding pages, inserting content blocks, and publishing. Share this with students starting their first blog.

Google Sites

Add and edit content in Google Sites

Covers inserting text, images, embedded Docs, YouTube videos, and Drive files into a Sites page. This is the core skill students need for rich blog posts.

Google Sites

Publish and share your site

Explains publishing options, custom page names, and link sharing. Important for deciding what your students' blogs are visible to and how parents can access them.

3. Google Classroom: Managing the Blogging Assignment

If you are running a blogging project across a group of students, Google Classroom is how you keep it organised. You can set a blogging assignment, collect draft Docs, leave private feedback, and track who has submitted without chasing anyone individually.

Google Classroom

Create an assignment in Google Classroom

How to set up an assignment, attach a template Doc, set a due date, and distribute it to all students with individual copies. The correct starting point for any blogging project.

Google Classroom

Give feedback on student work

Covers leaving private comments, returning work with grades, and using the comment bank for frequently used feedback phrases. Saves significant time when reviewing multiple blog drafts.

Google Classroom

Use the originality reports feature

Runs a similarity check on submitted writing and flags passages that match web content. Useful for encouraging authentic student voice in blog posts.

4. Google Drive: Organising Media and Assets

A student's blog will quickly accumulate images, audio recordings, and supporting documents. Keeping these organised in Drive from the start saves headaches later and teaches students good digital file management habits.

Google Drive

Organise files with folders in Google Drive

A simple but important guide. Encourage students to create a dedicated folder structure for their blog: one folder per post, with a subfolder for images and one for drafts.

Google Drive

Share files and folders in Google Drive

Explains the difference between sharing with specific people, sharing via link, and setting editor or viewer permissions. Foundational knowledge for student blogging workflows.

5. Google Slides: Visual Storytelling Within Blog Posts

Not every blog post needs to be text-only. Google Slides can be embedded directly into a Google Sites page, making it easy to add illustrated summaries, step-by-step guides, or photo essays alongside written content. Students often find Slides more approachable than a blank Doc when they first start telling a story visually.

Google Slides

Get started with Google Slides

Covers creating a presentation, adding slides, inserting images and text, and sharing. A useful reference for students creating a visual companion to their written blog post.

Google Slides

Embed a Slides presentation in Google Sites

Part of the Sites "Add content" guide, this section specifically covers embedding a Presentation block. Students can publish a Slides deck directly inside their blog post with one click.

6. YouTube: Creating and Embedding Video Posts

For older students, a vlog-style video post is a natural extension of blogging. A student records a short piece to camera or a screen recording, uploads it to a school YouTube channel or their own, and embeds it into their Google Sites blog. This combination works particularly well for science experiments, book reviews, or language practice posts.

YouTube

Upload videos to YouTube

Step-by-step guide to uploading a video, setting privacy settings (Unlisted is ideal for student projects), and getting the shareable link. Keep it Unlisted so only people with the link can view it.

YouTube

Embed YouTube videos in other websites

Explains how to get the embed code and drop it into any web page, including a Google Sites blog. Students can use this to publish a video post without making their channel fully public.

7. Google Forms: Peer Feedback on Blog Posts

Peer review is one of the most valuable parts of a blogging project and often the most awkward to run online. A short Google Form, shared with students after they have read each other's posts, structures the feedback so comments are constructive rather than vague. A well-designed form might ask students to name one thing they understood clearly, one question the post raised for them, and one suggestion for improvement.

Google Forms

Create a form in Google Forms

Getting started guide for building a Form from scratch. Covers adding questions, choosing answer types (short text, paragraph, rating scale), and sharing the form link with respondents.

Google Forms

View and manage Form responses

Explains how to see individual responses, view summary charts, and export responses to a Google Sheet. Useful for collecting all peer feedback in one place after a blogging assignment.

8. Whiteboard: Visual Brainstorming and Lesson Planning

Whiteboard tools are especially useful in tutoring sessions before a blog post is written. A student can sketch out their ideas, map a story structure, or organise a mind map with their tutor in real time before moving to Google Docs to write. It is also a natural way to brainstorm image ideas or plan a Slides visual.

A note on Google Jamboard: Google Jamboard, which was previously the go-to whiteboard tool in Workspace, was shut down on 31 December 2024. If you used it before, you will need to switch to one of the alternatives below.

What to use now:

Google Meet

Use the whiteboard in Google Meet

Google Meet now has a built-in whiteboard you can open during any video call. It is the simplest option for live tutoring sessions as it requires no additional sign-up, launches directly from Meet, and all participants can draw and annotate together in real time.

Google Workspace Marketplace

Whiteboard apps on Google Workspace Marketplace

Search results for whiteboard tools that integrate directly with Google Workspace. Top options include FigJam, Miro, and Lucidspark, all of which have free tiers suitable for individual tutors and small programmes.

FigJam

FigJam by Figma (free for educators)

The most commonly recommended Jamboard replacement for classroom use. FigJam boards are easy to share via link, support sticky notes, drawing, shapes, and emoji reactions, and the free education plan covers individual teachers and small groups. Works well for pre-writing brainstorming sessions.

Whiteboard in a blogging workflow: Before a student starts drafting, open a whiteboard in your Google Meet session and spend 5 to 10 minutes mapping the post together: what is the main point, what are two or three supporting ideas, what image or example fits each section. Students who plan visually first almost always write a more structured first draft.

Bonus: Google Teacher Center

If you want to go deeper on any of these tools, Google's own Teacher Center is the best single destination. It offers self-paced training courses, printable cheat sheets, and lesson ideas organised by subject and age group, all free and built by educators.

Google for Education

Google Teacher Center

The official hub for Google's educator training resources. Browse courses by tool or by skill level. Completing the Educator Level 1 course is a good foundation for any teacher using Workspace regularly.

Google for Education

Google Workspace for Education overview

Explains the different tiers of Workspace for Education, what is included in the free tier, and how schools can apply. Useful if you are advising a school or programme on setting up accounts for students.

A simple blogging workflow to get started: Student drafts in Google Docs → tutor leaves comments and returns it → student revises and publishes to Google Sites → peers complete a Google Form with feedback → student writes a short reflection at the top of the next post. That loop, repeated across a term, produces measurable writing improvement and a portfolio the student is genuinely proud of.

Final Thought

You do not need to use every tool at once. Start with Google Docs for drafting and Google Sites for publishing. Once that flow is familiar, layer in Classroom for assignment management, Forms for peer feedback, and Slides or YouTube for multimedia posts. Build the complexity gradually, and your students will build confidence alongside it.

Grade K to Grade 9: A Diaspora Parent’s Guide to the Nigerian Curriculum

8 July 2026 · 7 min read · By ThinkViva

You are at a school parents’ evening in Sydney, and your son’s teacher praises his reading ability. On the drive home, he mentions that his cousin in Lagos is already doing something called “Basic Science” and asks why his school does not teach it that way. You realise you have no clear answer — and no easy way to explain the difference between the two systems, or whether he is ahead, behind, or simply on a different track. If that conversation sounds familiar, this guide is for you.

Understanding the Nigerian school system can feel daunting from abroad, especially when your child’s host-country school uses entirely different terminology and structures. This article maps the Nigerian curriculum from Kindergarten through to Grade 9, explaining what your child should know at each stage and how diaspora families can stay meaningfully connected to it.

How the Nigerian School System Is Structured

Nigeria follows a 9-3-4 educational structure at the basic and secondary levels. For the purposes of diaspora families with children of school age, the section that matters most breaks down as follows:

  • Pre-Primary (Nursery and Kindergarten): Ages 3 to 5 — foundational literacy, numeracy, and social development
  • Primary School (Primary 1 to Primary 6): Ages 6 to 11 — broadly equivalent to Grade 1 through Grade 6
  • Junior Secondary School (JSS 1 to JSS 3): Ages 12 to 14 — broadly equivalent to Grade 7 through Grade 9

At the end of JSS 3 (Grade 9), pupils sit the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE), which marks the transition to Senior Secondary School. Understanding this Grade system in Nigeria is the essential first step to knowing where your child stands and what they need to cover.

Kindergarten: Building the Foundation

At the Kindergarten stage, the Nigerian curriculum focuses on pre-literacy and pre-numeracy skills: recognising letters and numbers, phonics, colours, shapes, and basic social interaction. Nigerian Kindergarten also introduces mother-tongue language — Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa depending on the family’s heritage — far earlier than most host-country schools do. For diaspora families, this early window is an excellent time to begin heritage language sessions, when children are still at their most linguistically receptive and learning a new language feels natural rather than effortful.

Primary 1 to Primary 3 (Grade 1 to Grade 3): Core Skills

The lower primary years consolidate the basics. Children work on English Language (reading and composition), Mathematics (the four operations, simple fractions, and early word problems), Basic Science (observations about the natural world), Social Studies, and their chosen Nigerian language. The Nigerian curriculum at this level is deliberately sequential — each year builds explicitly on the last, which means a child who misses a year of Nigerian curriculum content can fall behind in ways that are not always visible on their host-country school report. A child thriving in Grade 2 in Canada may have gaps in the Nigerian Primary 2 scheme of work that only become apparent later.

Primary 4 to Primary 6 (Grade 4 to Grade 6): Expanding Knowledge

Upper primary introduces more complex concepts across all subjects. Mathematics moves into long division, percentages, fractions of quantities, and introductory geometry. English comprehension becomes more demanding, and formal essay writing is introduced with specific structural expectations. Basic Science expands into simple machines, the human body, and environmental science. Social Studies covers Nigerian communities, civic responsibilities, and geography in greater depth.

This is frequently the stage at which diaspora parents reach out for support. By Primary 4 or 5, the gap between the Nigerian curriculum and a child’s host-country schooling has usually widened enough to be noticeable — but the child is still young enough that the gaps close quickly with the right tutor. A visit to the ThinkViva homepage at this stage can save considerable catch-up effort later.

JSS 1 to JSS 3 (Grade 7 to Grade 9): Junior Secondary School

Junior Secondary School is where the Nigerian curriculum demands the most careful attention from diaspora families — because it ends in a national examination. The JSS years cover a full academic breadth:

  • English Language: Summary writing, formal letter writing, comprehension, oral English, and speech work
  • Mathematics: Algebra, geometry, statistics, indices, and introductory trigonometry
  • Basic Science: Biology, chemistry, and physics threads woven into an integrated science syllabus
  • Social Studies and Civic Education: Nigerian governance, constitutional history, and responsible citizenship
  • Business Studies: Basic bookkeeping, commerce concepts, and introductory office skills
  • Cultural and Creative Arts: Music, visual arts, and drama with Nigerian cultural contexts
  • Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa: Continued language development in reading, writing, and oral expression

A child who has followed the Nigerian curriculum Grade 1 to Grade 9 consistently — with good tutoring support alongside their host-country schooling — will arrive at the BECE well prepared. A child who joins the Nigerian curriculum for the first time at JSS 2 or JSS 3 will need intensive, focused catch-up work. The earlier support begins, the smoother the journey.

How the Nigerian Grade System Compares Abroad

One of the most common questions diaspora parents ask is how Nigeria’s grades map onto the school systems in the UK, Canada, USA, and Australia. Here is a quick reference:

Nigeria UK Canada / USA Australia
Kindergarten Reception Kindergarten Foundation
Primary 1 to 6 Years 2 to 7 Grades 1 to 6 Years 1 to 6
JSS 1 to JSS 3 Years 8 to 10 Grades 7 to 9 Years 7 to 9

These are approximate alignments — the curriculum content at each level does not always match perfectly — but they give a useful starting point for understanding where your child sits across both systems simultaneously.

Keeping Your Child Connected to the Nigerian Curriculum

Staying aligned with the Nigerian curriculum from abroad does not require your child to repeat what they already know. A good tutor will assess your child’s current level in each subject, identify any gaps relative to the Nigerian curriculum, and build a session plan that fills those gaps efficiently without duplicating content already mastered in their host-country school. Two sessions per week of 60 to 90 minutes each is typically sufficient to maintain solid curriculum alignment at any grade level from Kindergarten to Grade 9.

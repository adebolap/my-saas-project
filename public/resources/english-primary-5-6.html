Part 1: Advanced Vocabulary & Figurative Language

Question 1 · Precision Verbs
Contextual Vocabulary
“Femi attempted to ___________ the heavy workload of his new international curriculum by organising a detailed daily study schedule.”
  • (a) mitigate
  • (b) exacerbate
  • (c) fabricate
  • (d) replicate
(a) mitigate — Means to make something less severe. Organising a study schedule reduces the difficulty of the workload, not increases it (exacerbate) or copies it (replicate).
Question 2 · Figurative Language
Metaphor & Literary Technique
When the teacher described the historical archives as “a goldmine of forgotten voices,” what literary technique was used, and what does it imply?
  • (a) Personification — the library is made of gold.
  • (b) Simile — the library looks like a mine.
  • (c) Metaphor — the archives contain extremely valuable historical records of people whose stories were lost.
  • (d) Hyperbole — nobody has ever visited the library.
(c) Metaphor — The archives are directly compared to a “goldmine” (valuable resource). It implies the records contain precious, undervalued history. No “like” or “as” = not a simile.
Question 3 · Root Words
Etymology: The Root “chron”
The Latin root “chron” means time (as in chronological). Based on this, what does the word anachronism most likely mean?
  • (a) Something happening at exactly the right time
  • (b) Something belonging to a time period other than where it exists
  • (c) Running out of time
  • (d) Predicting the future
(b) Something belonging to a time period other than where it exists — “Ana-” means against or back. An anachronism is something out of its correct time (e.g. a Viking using a smartphone).
Part 2: Reading Comprehension & Subtext Analysis

Read the passage carefully and answer in detailed sentences, using evidence from the text.

The Archive of Footsteps
Eleven-year-old Zara traced her finger along the spine of her grandfather’s worn leather journal, resting on her desk in London. Inside were handwritten notes documenting his migration journey from Lagos in the 1970s — recounting details of currency changes, shifting accents, and the quiet resilience required to build a life across two distinct hemispheres.

To Zara’s history teacher, the journal was a primary source document. To Zara, it was a living map. While her classmates struggled to understand historical migration through abstract textbook maps, Zara understood it through the ink stains and margin notes of her own lineage. She realised that history wasn’t just a collection of dates stored in a classroom; it was a continuous conversation between where you came from and where you were standing.
Question 4 · Literal Retrieval
Two Different Views
According to the text, how does Zara’s history teacher view the journal, and how does Zara view it differently?
The teacher views it as a primary source document (historical evidence). Zara views it as “a living map” — a personal connection to her family’s history and identity, not just an academic resource.
Question 5 · Inference & Analysis
Reading the Deeper Meaning
Explain what the author means by the phrase: “history wasn’t just a collection of dates stored in a classroom; it was a continuous conversation between where you came from and where you were standing.”
The author means that history is personal and active. Our past experiences and heritage directly shape our identity and how we see the world today. History lives in people, not just textbooks. Award marks for identifying the idea that heritage influences present identity.
Question 6 · Metaphor
Bridging Two Worlds
Identify one descriptive metaphor used in the passage that bridges Zara’s dual reality as a diaspora child.
“A living map” — The journal is called a “living map” because it navigates both her grandfather’s past and her present identity. It connects Lagos (origin) to London (present), like a map connecting two places.
Part 3: Grammar & Sentence Transformation

Refine the sentences below to meet Grade 5–6 academic writing standards.

Question 7 · Active Voice
Active vs. Passive Voice
Original (passive): “The historical archive was carefully examined by Zara.”

Rewrite this sentence in the active voice to make it more direct.
“Zara carefully examined the historical archive.”
The subject (Zara) now performs the action, making the sentence direct and punchy. Passive voice buries the actor; active voice leads with them.
Question 8 · Relative Clauses
Combining Sentences
Combine these two sentences into one sophisticated sentence using a relative clause starting with “which” or “whose”:

Sentence A: Zara read her grandfather’s journal.
Sentence B: The journal contained detailed notes about his migration.
“Zara read her grandfather’s journal, which contained detailed notes about his migration.”
The relative clause (“which contained...”) is embedded mid-sentence, adding detail without starting a new sentence. Accept variations using “whose pages contained...” also.
Part 4: Analytical Essay Challenge

This is an open-ended writing task. Use the parent checklist to evaluate your child’s response.

Essay Prompt (90–120 words)
The Value of Heritage in Learning
Write a well-structured paragraph arguing whether a student’s personal cultural background helps them better understand school subjects like history or literature.

Your paragraph must include at least one academic transition word (e.g. furthermore, consequently, conversely) and back up your argument with a logical reason.
Parent & Tutor Evaluation Checklist
  • Features a clear, arguable thesis statement in the opening sentence.
  • Uses complex punctuation correctly (semicolons, introductory clause commas).
  • Employs precise upper-primary vocabulary (e.g. perspective, primary source, lineage, analysis).
  • Includes at least one academic transition word and a logical supporting reason.
  • Paragraph is coherent, stays on topic, and reaches a conclusion.
